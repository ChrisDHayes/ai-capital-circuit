The AI Capital Circuit

Capital formation, circular financing, industrial capacity and the question of durable economic value

Research essay and methodological companion to the interactive financial observatory
Christopher Hayes, CDAIO · MBAN · MPS DAV · MSc in AI Candidate, Boston University
Evidence cutoff: October 28, 2026 · Prepared for publication: October 30, 2026

The question is not whether AI investment is large, interconnected or ambitious. It is whether capital, productive capacity, commercial demand and financial obligations are moving toward an economically sustainable equilibrium.

Explore the interactive investigation · Read the Substack article

Executive argument

The financing of artificial intelligence is commonly presented as a collection of investments, capital-expenditure announcements and complex arrows between recognizable companies. Those diagrams reveal something important: a set of investors, cloud providers, AI developers, semiconductor firms and specialized infrastructure operators are becoming more financially interdependent. But the diagrams can also obscure the economic substance of the relationships they depict. An investment, borrowing facility, forward capacity purchase, supplier prepayment, secondary share sale, recognized revenue and enterprise valuation are fundamentally different measures.

The AI Capital Circuit reconstructs the ecosystem as four linked systems: (1) capital origins, (2) financing and commercial obligations, (3) productive physical deployment and (4) realized economic demand. Over these sits a fifth layer, the history of what information was publicly available at each point in time. The system is studied with seven narrative questions, using a curated sample of companies and disclosed relationships rather than claiming to model every actor or dollar.

The core hypothesis is conditional. Increasing financial interconnectedness may help coordinate the construction of essential AI capacity and support genuine commercialization. The same arrangement can, under some circumstances, amplify dependence on a small number of counterparties, expose infrastructure borrowers to refinancing pressure and obscure whether the ultimate economic value has caught up with contractual expectations. Neither inference should be assumed before tracing the instruments, dates and observable outcomes.

The research therefore rejects two shortcuts. Circularity is not proof of cash recycling, and rapid revenue growth is not proof of attractive returns on capital. The analysis is most useful when it can show evidence for each proposition simultaneously.

1. The seven-theme analytical narrative

Theme One: The acceleration — How much is being invested, and what is counted?

Across five large technology companies, Samaritan Research’s SEC-derived aggregate indicates that broad corporate capital expenditure increased from about $162 billion in 2022 to about $448 billion in 2025, using the compiler’s stated treatment of cash property-and-equipment additions and new finance leases. These are company-wide totals; not all outlays can be attributed uniquely to AI. [1] The difference matters because an aggregate described as “AI investment” can combine AI compute infrastructure with broader cloud, logistics, facilities and other corporate investments.

This acceleration signals a structural shift toward a more capital-intensive industrial system. The commitment to deploy computing hardware requires land, power, networking equipment, cooling, specialized construction, supply-chain coordination and continuing technology replacement. Accounting treatment also matters: a finance lease is not always the same as a cash payment in the measurement period; a commitment to purchase hardware is not necessarily current capital spending; spending on a data centre does not indicate when that capacity generates revenue.

The first visualization must therefore answer three related questions: What has already been spent? What has been committed for the future? And what physical capacity has been delivered? These require different datasets and should never be stacked as though they were additive cash transfers.

Theme Two: The origins of capital — Who funds the buildout?

Capital enters the ecosystem through internally generated cash flows, public or private equity, corporate bonds, secured credit, private credit, asset-backed financing, joint ventures and sometimes public-sector incentives. The origin determines who is exposed, how funding costs respond to macroeconomic conditions and whether the recipient has continuing contractual repayment obligations.

Microsoft’s FY2026 filing makes the distinction tangible. Microsoft reported a total OpenAI funding commitment of $13.0 billion, with $11.9 billion funded as of June 30, 2026. The same filing reports $24.1 billion of revenue from OpenAI commercial arrangements, including revenue-sharing payments, and $6.0 billion of receivables. [2] The three amounts convey different things: capital invested or committed, revenue recognized and receivables outstanding. It would be wrong to combine them as new capital invested; equally wrong to assume the related-party revenue is entirely compute spending by OpenAI.

SoftBank’s October 2026 announcement gives a useful contrasting example: the completion of a $30 billion follow-on investment through three tranches is an actual funding milestone, while the instruments SoftBank used to fund its own investment belong to a separate financing chain. [4] This is a case where transaction status changes the interpretation of a headline. A stated intention and a completed cash investment should not carry the same visual encoding.

The BIS has examined the increasing role of borrowing within the AI infrastructure buildout, including the use of entities and arrangements that tie financing to assets and customer commitments. [8][9] The existence of such financing does not in itself demonstrate distress. It does mean that credit capacity, maturity dates, collateral, covenants and the identity of the economic risk-bearer become important parts of the story.

Theme Three: The financial network — Who funds, buys, supplies and guarantees?

The visual language of an ecosystem network is appealing because it makes concentration immediately visible. But the financial meaning of its circles and arrows must be controlled. A company’s market value is not capital received. The face value of a long-term compute agreement is not supplier revenue recognized in the current year. The amount of an arranged credit facility is not necessarily the amount actually lent by each named financial institution.

In September 2025, NVIDIA and OpenAI announced a plan involving potential investment of up to $100 billion alongside major GPU infrastructure deployment. This represented an announced strategic intention subject to milestones, not proof of $100 billion of funds transferred. [3] A network that draws a completed investment arrow of that size would materially distort the picture.

CoreWeave’s 2025 announcements involving OpenAI illustrate another failure mode. An initial agreement and subsequent expansions brought the reported combined contract value to approximately $22.4 billion. [5] Incremental increases and the cumulative total refer to the same evolving contractual relationship; adding all headlines together without identifying parent agreements will double count value.

The network should carry at least six attributes per relationship: sender/obligor, recipient/beneficiary, instrument type, economic magnitude and basis, effective or recognition date, and evidence status. More elaborate legal entity, collateral or repayment features are attached where disclosed. A connection may be present even when its amount is unknown. Unknown is not zero.

Network concentration is not the same as cash recycling

Three distinct claims must not collapse into one:

  1. Connected: Two organizations have a documented financial or commercial relationship.
  2. Dependent: That relationship represents a material share of funding, revenue, capacity or exposure for one party.
  3. Recycled cash: Identifiable funding proceeds financed related purchases and returned through a traceable chain.

The first is relatively easy to establish. The second requires a denominator and a time period. The third requires evidence of actual funding use and cash movement. The visual similarity of a closed loop is not proof of the third proposition. This is a central research safeguard.

Theme Four: Company trajectories — How do financial models diverge over time?

A single financial network captures connections; it does not show whether business models are changing in the same direction. A time-evolving bubble plot can expose the differences: revenue growth on the horizontal axis, cash capex relative to revenue vertically, bubble area as revenue scale, and colour as company cohort. These variables are meaningfully interpretable for firms with consistent annual financial statements, but less so for private model developers using selective revenue run-rate reporting.

The initial public-company historical panel makes it possible to show movement for a curated subset without filling every year with estimates. The sample is not a universal ranking. The accounting and fiscal-year boundaries matter: Microsoft and Oracle do not close their fiscal years on December 31; the underlying definition of cash capital spending is not uniform across all issuers. Therefore, year selectors must show fiscal period-end dates and the exact metric definition.

The bubble chart should communicate trajectories rather than award a verdict. High investment intensity can indicate a speculative buildout, but it can also indicate a deliberate phase of infrastructure creation. Low investment intensity may reflect an asset-light business model, not automatically a stronger competitive position. Context—cash generation, customer contract structure, utilization and return on capital—is needed.

Theme Five: The return of demand — When do promises become cash-generating services?

Commercial revenue is the necessary bridge between capital formation and sustainable economics. Yet even revenue is not the final measure of investment quality. A company may report substantial recognized sales while remaining dependent on external financing, uneconomic customer acquisition or concentrated obligations.

CoreWeave’s FY2025 Form 10-K reported about $60.7 billion in remaining performance obligations, compared with $15.1 billion at the end of 2024. Its largest customer accounted for 67% of annual revenue. [6] This combination is instructive: future contracted services may offer visibility, while concentration introduces exposure if a large counterparty changes strategy, utilization or delivery requirements. Remaining performance obligations are not identical to cash already collected or guaranteed profits. They are an accounting measure of contracted, unsatisfied performance subject to the agreement’s terms and the company’s accounting policies.

The economic conversion chain is useful to visualize explicitly:

Financing → equipment ordered → capacity delivered and energized → capacity utilized → service delivered → invoice/receivable → cash collection → operating contribution → return on invested capital.

Each arrow involves its own failure modes. Equipment can be delayed; capacity may be delivered but underutilized; service can be recognized as revenue before payment; refinancing may be needed before the asset’s productive life ends. The IEA’s reporting on energy and AI makes power supply, grid access and infrastructure timing relevant to this financial sequence. [10]

A counterargument deserves equal weight: advance contracting can be a rational way to coordinate an industrial buildout. When it is priced properly and supported by creditworthy buyers, it can reduce uncertainty for investors and lenders. The danger is not advance contracting as such; it is assuming contractual demand will translate automatically into realized economic returns at the originally expected scale and price.

Theme Six: Obligations and dependencies — Where can constraints propagate?

The financial system does not become fragile simply because it contains debt. The relevant questions are maturity, contractual cash generation, financing headroom, collateral adequacy, interest costs, guarantees, refinancing options and the concentration of revenue and credit relationships.

For example, an infrastructure borrower may rely on contracted capacity payments to support a secured credit facility. A customer delay could affect operating cash flow, a minimum debt-service coverage calculation, the ability to make distributions and the appetite of lenders to finance additional capacity. But it takes specific contract language to determine whether a cash-trap condition applies; it takes reporting evidence to determine whether any covenant was actually breached.

The research evolved from a firm-level debt snapshot to more granular instruments, subsidiaries, collateral roles, payment priorities, historical contract amendments and covenant event histories. That matters because the holder of the actual economic exposure is not necessarily the institution identified as an arranger or administrative agent. A credit agreement can distinguish between an ordinary operating cash waterfall and post-enforcement distributions; these are not interchangeable insolvency recovery orders.

CoreWeave’s filings and financing documentation provide a practical basis for selected debt and covenant case studies. [7] The BIS’s 2026 work provides the wider financial-system context, while also noting that observed bond-market spillovers from the events it studied were small and short-lived. [8] This counterevidence should not be hidden behind an alarming visual metaphor.

Theme Seven: The sustainability test — Can commercial outcomes carry the capital system?

No single number resolves this question. A company with strong operating cash flow can sustain heavy capital expenditures for years, but may still earn a poor incremental return. A younger infrastructure company can have rapid revenue growth and substantial contracted demand, yet face refinancing or customer concentration risks. A model provider can grow revenue rapidly while maintaining undisclosed compute costs and uncertain profitability.

The right synthesis is a balance of evidence rather than a bubble score:

  • Is independent paying-customer demand increasing, and can it be distinguished from strategically affiliated transactions?
  • Is capacity being deployed and utilized at economic rates?
  • Are revenue, cash collections and operating contributions growing relative to incremental capital intensity?
  • How concentrated are customers, suppliers, capital sources and debt maturities?
  • Are funding commitments being closed and drawn, or simply announced?
  • Are amendments and financing restructurings improving flexibility or reflecting underlying pressure—and what direct evidence supports either interpretation?
  • What is unknown because private companies disclose insufficient financial detail?

A credible research product may end in uncertainty. That is a strength if the uncertainty is structured, sourced and falsifiable. The question is whether the system’s underlying economics are improving as quickly as the capital and obligations supporting them grow.

2. Five alternative hypotheses and how we would test them

H1. Productive industrial capital formation

Claim: Large investment is building durable infrastructure for a growing market. Supporting evidence: rising independently generated customer revenue, stronger utilization, falling unit costs, cash conversion and eventual returns on invested capital. Disconfirming evidence: persistent underutilization, repeated refinancing on deteriorating terms or impairments despite scale growth.

H2. Supplier-supported commercialization

Claim: Strategic supplier investments help customers fund infrastructure procurement, creating dependence. Supporting evidence: linked financing and purchase agreements, material customer concentration, disclosed use of proceeds and actual related-party cash flows. Important limitation: related-party commercial revenue can be legitimate revenue; the existence of a loop does not prove fictitious demand or inappropriate accounting.

H3. Migration of financial risk into credit structures

Claim: As capital requirements rise, financing moves toward debt, secured loans, lease obligations and project vehicles. Supporting evidence: growth in outstanding principal, material maturity schedules and additional collateral or covenant restrictions. Counterevidence: strong cash balances, diversified loan syndicates, long contracted revenue visibility and manageable maturities. BIS research contextualizes the transition without establishing a realized crisis. [8][9]

H4. Concentration amid apparent diversification

Claim: The network may add nodes and edges yet remain vulnerable to a small number of central customers or infrastructure suppliers. Testing: named revenue concentration, investor dependence and common credit-exposure measures with appropriate denominators. Caution: an institution named in multiple arrangements may act only as arranger; lender exposure amounts are often unavailable.

H5. Misaligned economic clocks

Claim: Financing and physical deployment commitments may mature before the related infrastructure generates sufficient return. Testing: funding dates, construction completion, energization, contract start, utilization, collections, debt maturities, GPU replacement cycles and depreciation policies. Caution: these measures are often available on different bases or only for selected projects.

3. Four ledgers and a time-aware evidence model

To prevent misleading arithmetic, the analysis maintains four linked record types.

Capital origins ledger. Primary equity, secondary ownership transfers, debt issuance and drawn debt, undrawn commitments, capital contributions, public subsidies and operating cash used for investment. A secondary sale changes shareholders without necessarily adding operating capital to the issuer. Undrawn borrowing capacity is not cash.

Commercial and financing obligation ledger. Contract value, take-or-pay commitments, remaining performance obligations, debt principal and maturities, contractual minimums, guarantees, service conditions and options. Every commitment must be marked by status, enforceability, parties and relevant dates.

Realized economics ledger. Recognized revenue, billed and collected cash, operating cash flow, free cash flow under explicit definitions, cash PP&E additions, depreciation, operating margin and other reported results. Run rates must never be substituted silently for audited annual figures.

Physical deployment ledger. Data-centre capacity planned or delivered, megawatts energized, GPU capacity installed or contracted, utilization where disclosed, delivery and commissioning dates, asset useful lives and power availability. Do not infer these quantities from contract values alone.

Historical knowledge layer. Each record retains (a) event date, (b) publication date, (c) economic measurement date, (d) financial recognition or cash settlement date where known, and (e) later revisions or supersession links. This enables a difference between an ‘as-known-at-the-time’ view and a retrospective updated view. A proposed investment must not appear as a completed cash transfer merely because the eventual deal was announced later.

4. Information design choices and their meaning

A financial constellation, not an unlabeled network

The opening network deliberately takes inspiration from rich financial ecosystem diagrams, but its visual encodings are explicit. Circle area may represent public market capitalization or an eligible disclosed private valuation on a dated basis; other modes use completed financing, commercial contract face values, debt principal or observed connectivity. The value of a company, the amount of its obligations and its systemic importance are distinct. Link colour conveys instrument type; link width conveys magnitude only when an eligible comparable number exists. Edge direction represents the legal or economic relationship, not an assertion about end-to-end cash tracing.

Animated company movement with transparent denominators

A bubble’s horizontal position can show year-on-year revenue growth; its vertical position, annual cash capex divided by revenue; its area, reported annual revenue; its colour, cohort. Missing values are missing. Selection trails are shown only where valid historical observations exist, and ‘latest disclosed year’ views prominently show the observation dates. Fiscal 2026 does not automatically mean calendar 2026.

Directed flows instead of mixed-dollar Sankeys

Equity funding, debt drawn, future compute commitments and supplier revenue should not be mixed in one continuous flow with a shared dollar denominator. The source-backed flow explorer shows separate instrument views. A genuinely comprehensive cash-flow Sankey would require traceable origins, destinations, settlement dates and accounting eliminations that are not available for the entire market.

Financial clocks and stress analysis

Maturity bars, liquidity observations, financial covenant thresholds and stress sensitivities can illuminate possible constraints. However, assumptions used in a laboratory must be visually distinct from reported facts. A calculated hypothetical residual is not a solvency forecast; a hypothetical DSCR shortfall is not evidence of a breached loan covenant. A documented amendment is not by itself evidence of default.

5. A financial integrity checklist

The following rules govern both the writing and the software:

  1. Do not add unlike instruments. A purchase commitment, equity investment, lending commitment and recognized revenue can be related but not aggregated as one flow.
  2. Do not double count cumulative and incremental announcements. Parent deal IDs and supersession dates should connect revisions to their source transactions.
  3. Separate contractual maxima from amounts realized. “Up to” is not “completed.” Undrawn capacity is not available cash without qualifications.
  4. Date every valuation. Public market cap and private financing valuation are different measurement types and may refer to different points in time.
  5. Preserve issuer-versus-subsidiary identities. A parent’s guarantee may be limited or absent; an administrative agent does not necessarily bear the full credit risk.
  6. Never infer lender allocations from agent lists. Named syndicate participants do not imply equal or known holdings.
  7. Avoid false historical precision. As-of and publication dates are distinct, and missing years are not filled merely to animate a chart.
  8. Retain counterevidence. A compliance statement, cash-flow strength or small observed bond spillover belongs alongside a financing-risk hypothesis.
  9. Explain source gaps. Unverified private-company financials should not become hard quantitative visual encodings.
  10. Disclose editorial inference. Analysis must be labeled separately from observed facts and hypothetical scenarios.

6. What we can say now — and what remains unresolved

The evidence supports an extraordinary acceleration in broad technology capital expenditure, material relationships between major infrastructure buyers and sellers, an expanding range of secured financing structures and significant heterogeneity among individual companies. Microsoft is an example of very large internal cash generation alongside complex related-party commercial and equity links. CoreWeave’s historical customer concentration and RPO demonstrate why forward demand and risk concentration must be studied together. [1][2][6]

The evidence does not support a single dollar estimate of cash recycled around the entire AI economy; a verified global network of all equity, debt and contracting relationships; an across-the-board claim of covenant stress; or a calibrated default-contagion model. Even comprehensive public filings do not disclose every private lender allocation, end-customer economics or specific use of financing proceeds. The datasets are a transparent curated sample, not a census.

The IMF’s October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report provides further context for market concentration, leverage and asset-pricing risks. [12] The BIS’s October 2026 paper identifies financing structures and warns that changes in market supply and shocks could have wider effects, while reporting limited short-lived spillovers during its studied event window. [8] This evidence should restrain both alarmism and complacency.

7. The broader significance: what the industry is becoming

The AI economy is not merely a software adoption cycle. It includes industrial capital formation, contracted computing capacity, semiconductor supply chains, energy access, financial intermediation and the commercialization of models and applications. Its most interesting challenge is that the financial and technological components evolve on different schedules.

One possibility is that these institutions collectively build infrastructure that unlocks productivity and new markets, with commercial demand ultimately supporting the capital deployed. Another is that parts of the ecosystem overbuild, refinance repeatedly, or disappoint expectations even while AI adoption continues to increase. Different parts of the chain can have different outcomes. Semiconductor firms, cloud providers, infrastructure operators, model developers and end-user businesses do not share identical exposures or value-capture dynamics.

The analytical ambition is therefore not to pronounce the entire sector sound or unsound. It is to identify the conditions under which the financial circuit grows stronger or weaker: diversified independent demand, sustainable returns, resilient financing terms, adaptable infrastructure and transparency about obligations. The failure to distinguish enthusiasm from economic performance is itself a risk in interpreting technological change.

8. How to use and update the interactive research

The seven-theme experience is designed for two levels of readership. A general audience can read the story sequentially; technical readers can inspect the financing classifications, company movements, deal lifecycle, debt ladders, legal recourse and covenant evidence. The source desk points back to filings and announcements.

The product can be updated as filings appear, but there is no live data feed or continuously operating monitoring service in the standalone HTML. A candidate new observation should be logged with an accessible source, compared with the current record, assigned a status, reviewed by a human and only then incorporated into a published dataset. Old observations remain preserved when a deal is amended or a historical claim is superseded.

Recommended update cadence: review material issuer filings around earnings periods; update transaction and source registers on major deals; maintain a dated release log; rerun source-link, arithmetic, anchor and accessibility checks before publishing a new version. Annual or quarterly figures should only change when a disclosed observation actually changes.

9. Methodological limits and publication statement

This is a public-information analytical project, not a valuation opinion or investment recommendation. Figures are in nominal US dollars unless otherwise stated. Dataset dates and filing periods are carried through the underlying release; quoted totals may be rounded. Private financial figures and financing valuations can be less complete or less comparable than audited public financial statements. The interactive interface distinguishes observed values, commitments, contractual claims and illustrative models to the extent permitted by available records.

A curated source register accompanies this essay. Individual financial statements, credit agreements and company announcements are primary evidence for transaction and accounting claims; independent research supplies broader interpretation. The exhibit source lists and archived study workbooks should be read together when a claim is consequential.

Appendix A — Financial terms worth distinguishing

Capital expenditure: Investment in property, equipment or other assets under a specified accounting definition. A company may report cash outlays and newly capitalized leases separately.

Equity financing / primary issuance: Funds received by an issuer in exchange for equity. Secondary transaction: Purchase of equity from an existing holder, not necessarily new issuer funding.

Debt commitment: Maximum agreed lending capacity subject to contract terms. Drawn debt: Funds borrowed and outstanding (or drawn during the period, as explicitly stated). Debt principal maturity: Contractual principal due under an applicable repayment schedule.

Commercial contract value: Agreed or announced face value across a contract period. Remaining performance obligations: Transaction consideration allocated to contracted goods/services not yet delivered, subject to the applicable accounting definition. Recognized revenue: Revenue recognized when the accounting criteria are satisfied, not automatically cash collected.

Annualized revenue run rate: Extrapolation from a shorter observation, not audited full-year revenue. Valuation: Market capitalization or privately negotiated financing valuation, not an amount invested or received.

Customer concentration: Share of revenue or other relevant business measure attributable to a customer. DSCR: A debt-service coverage calculation defined by a specific financing agreement, not necessarily equivalent to a generic accounting ratio.

Cash trap: Contractual restrictions on the release or distribution of cash under defined conditions. Equity cure: A mechanism, where permitted, for applying eligible sponsor/equity contributions to specified covenant or funding requirements. Enforcement waterfall: Contractual priority for distributing specified proceeds after enforcement, subject to relevant agreements and law.
